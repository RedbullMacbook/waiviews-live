Hamilton Lake

About this walk

Hamilton Lake (Lake Rotoroa) is the big lake on the south side of the city, about 10 minutes from the middle of town. The walkway goes all the way around it and is 3.8 km long, so it takes about an hour at a normal walking pace.

The track is flat and wide, a mix of sealed path and gravel, with no steps. That means it works for prams, wheelchairs and bikes as well as walkers. Around the lake there are playgrounds, picnic tables, toilets and water refill stations.

Video description: A 16 minute walk around Hamilton Lake. It starts on the lawn beside a large white building with a curved roof, goes past a playground and a rock water feature, then follows the path along the water's edge where ducks are swimming. After that it passes through a long stretch of flax, tall grasses and big trees, with houses across the water, and finishes on a grassy area with a bench.

Things to look out for

  • Ducks, pukeko and geese on the water and on the grass
  • The rock water feature next to the playground
  • Flax (harakeke) and tall grasses planted along the lake edge
  • Cabbage trees standing up against the sky
  • The houses on the other side of the lake, reflected in the water

Location: Ruakiwi Road and Lake Crescent, Hamilton

Time: About 1 hour (3.8 km loop)

Surface: Flat sealed path and gravel, no steps

Tips: Free to walk and open all year. Parking is free along Ruakiwi Road and near Innes Common but it fills up on weekends, so go early. Bring water and a hat.

A bit of history

Rotoroa is te reo Māori for "long lake". Before the town was built, the lake had native trees and plants growing around it. Here is how it changed over time:

  • After 1864: Military settlers cleared a lot of the native plants around the lake for farming and recreation. The western side ended up as a muddy swamp.
  • 1886: The Hamilton Domain Board took over the lake and the land on its west side, and started planting trees and building paths.
  • 1890s: People swam and had picnics at the lake, and brass bands played concerts.
  • 1950s: There was a plan to fill in the swamp with rubbish. Locals didn't want that, so they formed a "Lake Society" to protect the lake.
  • Later on: The council added toilets, playgrounds and wooden walkways.
  • 2004: A walkway that goes right around the whole lake was opened. This is the track in our video.

Walk guide: stop by stop

This matches the video, so you can skip to the part you want to see.

  1. 0:00 - The start: We begin on the lawn next to a big white building with a curved roof. This is a good place to park and set off.
  2. 1:00 - Playground: The path goes past a playground and a rock water feature. Kids love this part, and there are toilets and a water refill station nearby.
  3. 2:00 - The water's edge: A wide sealed path runs right next to the lake, with rocks along the edge. This is the best place to spot ducks.
  4. 3:00 - Lawns and big trees: The path opens out onto grass and tall trees. You can see the houses on the other side of the lake from here.
  5. 5:00 - Native planting: The path goes into a shady section with flax, tall grasses and trees growing on both sides. It is quieter here and feels a bit like a bush walk in the middle of the city.
  6. 8:00 - Through the grasses: A long stretch of native planting, with other walkers and runners ahead. The path winds around so you never see too far ahead.
  7. 11:00 - Houses across the water: The lake opens up again and the path curves between the tall grasses.
  8. 13:00 - Lake views: The water shows through the flax and cabbage trees stand out against the sky.
  9. 15:00 - Nearly there: We finish on a grassy area with a bench, a nice spot to sit down after the walk.

Plants and wildlife

The lake is home to lots of birds. You will almost always see ducks, and pukeko (the blue and red birds with long legs) and geese are common too, especially on the grass near the water.

A lot of the planting around the lake edge is native. Flax (harakeke) was used by Māori to weave baskets, mats and clothing, and the tall grasses and cabbage trees (tī kōuka) help make a home for birds and insects. Replanting the edges also stops mud washing into the water.

Did you know?

  • Hamilton Lake is also called Lake Rotoroa, and rotoroa means "long lake".
  • The track is 3.8 km, so walking it twice is nearly 8 km.
  • Early settlers once swam in the lake and held brass band concerts next to it.
  • The track is flat enough for prams and wheelchairs, so almost everyone can use it.
  • Hamilton City Council needs to approve drones before you fly one here.

Plan your visit

  • Early morning and late afternoon are the quietest times.
  • Dogs need to be on a lead. Innes Common nearby has a space where dogs can run off the lead.
  • Bikes are allowed, but watch out for people walking.
  • Bring sunscreen, a hat and some water. There is not much shade on the open parts.
  • There are picnic tables and playgrounds, so it works well for a family day out.
