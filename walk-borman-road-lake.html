Borman Road Lake

About this walk

Borman Road Lake is a wetland lake in Rototuna, in the north of Hamilton. It sits right in the middle of a newer part of the suburb, with houses all around it, but once you are on the path it feels surprisingly green and quiet.

The paths are a mix of concrete and fine gravel, and there are a few wooden bridges where you cross over the water. Most of it is flat, so it is an easy walk that you can do in your own time. Parts of the walk follow the footpath next to the road.

Video description: A 15 minute walk around Borman Road Lake on a cloudy day. It starts next to new houses looking over the wetland, follows a gravel path and then a concrete path winding through tussock grasses, crosses wooden bridges with green railings, goes along the footpath past a bus shelter, and finishes on a wooden deck looking out over the flax and grasses.

Things to look out for

  • The wooden bridges with green railings over the water
  • Native tussock grasses that change colour from green to orange and brown
  • Flax (harakeke) and cabbage trees growing around the water
  • Ducks and other birds hiding in the planting
  • How the wetland fits in between all the new houses

Location: Borman Road, Rototuna, Hamilton

Time: About 15 minutes at an easy pace

Surface: Concrete and gravel paths, wooden bridges, mostly flat

Tips: Some of the walk is next to the road, so keep an eye on kids and dogs. Bring a jacket as it can get windy out in the open.

What is a wetland lake?

Lakes like this one are built into new suburbs to deal with rainwater. When it rains, water runs off roofs, driveways and roads. Instead of all going straight into the streams, it flows into the wetland first.

In the wetland the water slows down, so dirt and rubbish can sink to the bottom. The plants also help clean the water before it moves on. This means cleaner streams, less flooding and a home for birds and insects, all in the middle of a suburb.

Plants and wildlife

Almost everything planted around the lake is native. The tussock grasses are the orange and brown clumps you see all through the video. Flax (harakeke) has long, strong leaves that Māori used for weaving, and cabbage trees (tī kōuka) are the tall ones with a spiky top.

Because the planting is thick, it is a good hiding spot for ducks and small birds. If you visit in person, walk slowly and quietly and you will hear more birds than you see.

Did you know?

  • Wetlands like this help stop flooding by holding rainwater and letting it out slowly.
  • The plants around the edge act like a filter and help clean the water.
  • This is the newest of our walks. The area around it has been built up in the last few years.
  • It is only a short walk, so it is a good one for a quick break or for little kids.

Plan your visit

  • There is no proper carpark, so park on the street and be respectful of the people who live there.
  • There is a bus stop on the route if you want to leave the car at home.
  • Stay on the paths and bridges, as the ground near the water can be soft.
  • Keep dogs on a lead because of the birds and the road.

Think about it

  • Why do you think new suburbs build wetlands like this?
  • How is this walk different from Hamilton Lake or the Waikato River?
  • What would this area have looked like before the houses were built?
